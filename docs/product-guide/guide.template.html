Product guide · everything built so far

A pharmacy chain that can prove where every tablet went.

This is inventory software for a multi-branch pharmacy — five locations, one central warehouse, medicines that expire and are recalled by batch. It tracks stock the way a bank tracks money: nothing is ever edited, only added. On top of that history sit four features that read the past to tell you something useful about the future — what to buy, how much, what looks wrong, and which supplier is actually slow.

What problem it solves

A pharmacy chain has a specific kind of pain that ordinary inventory software handles badly. Medicines have batch numbers and expiry dates, so two boxes of the same product are not interchangeable. Stock sits in five different places and gets moved between them. Some drugs are controlled and every gram has to be accounted for. When a manufacturer recalls a batch, you have hours — not days — to say exactly where every unit of it went.

So the system is built around three promises:

  • Nothing disappears. Every change to stock is a permanent, timestamped line in a ledger. You can always answer "why is this number what it is?"
  • Every unit has a batch. Stock is counted per product, per location, and per batch — so a recall is a lookup, not an investigation.
  • The computer explains itself. Every suggestion the system makes — order 190 of these, look at that Tuesday — comes with the arithmetic that produced it, in a panel you can open.

The one big idea

Stock is a ledger, not a number

Most inventory systems keep a column called quantity and change it. When the number turns out to be wrong, there is no way to find out how it got that way — the evidence was overwritten.

This system never does that. Receiving stock, selling it, moving it, throwing it away — each one appends a line. The quantity on hand is not stored as a fact; it is the sum of every line so far.

MovementChangeBalance
Goods receipt · batch PAR-650-B2+120120
Sale issue · Andheri Branch−5565
Transfer dispatch → Bandra Branch−1550
Adjustment · damaged in handling−347

Two consequences follow, and they are the reason the rest of the product can exist at all:

  • Mistakes are corrected, not erased. Getting a sale wrong means appending a reversing line. Both lines stay. The audit trail is the truth.
  • The past is complete. Because two years of every movement are genuinely on record, the forecasting and anomaly features have real material to work with. They are not guessing from a snapshot.

Where the fast numbers come from. Adding up 53,000 rows every time someone opens a page would be slow, so the database keeps a running total in a side table and updates it automatically the instant a movement is written. That total is a convenience, never the source of truth — it can always be rebuilt from the ledger, and a test checks that it still matches.

Overview · /

Dashboard

The first screen after signing in. It answers one question — is anything wrong right now? — and nothing else.

  • Four tiles across the top. Units on hand, how many products have dropped below their reorder level, how many batches expire within 30 days, and what the whole lot is worth.
  • A red banner when stock is frozen. Quarantined units — held under a recall or waiting for a quality check — are counted separately, because they are on the shelf but must not be dispensed.
  • Expiry watchlist. The batches closest to expiring, so they get sold first rather than written off later.
  • Recent activity. A live feed of the ledger. Every line here is a permanent record; green is stock coming in, grey is stock going out.

Inventory · /products

Products

The catalogue. One row per medicine, with the rules that govern how it is stored, sold and taxed.

A pharmacy product carries more rules than an ordinary retail item, and each one changes how the system behaves:

  • Drug schedule — India classifies medicines into schedules. H and H1 need a prescription; X is tightly controlled. The schedule decides how carefully the system protects that product's stock level.
  • Storage condition — room temperature, or cold chain. Cold-chain products are treated as critical, because a fridge failure costs the whole batch.
  • GST rate — India's sales tax. Stored per product so invoices split correctly into central and state portions without anyone doing arithmetic.
  • Pack size — a strip of 10, a box of 100. Order suggestions are always rounded up to whole packs, because a distributor cannot ship 7.3 of anything.

Inventory · /stock

Stock

What is physically where, right now — split by location, by batch, and by whether it is actually available to sell.

The important idea here is that "in stock" is not one number. The same 120 units can be in four different conditions, and only one of them can be handed to a patient:

StatusMeaningCan it be sold?
AvailableOn the shelf, unexpired, unrestrictedYes
QuarantinedHeld under a recall or waiting on a quality checkNo
In transitDispatched from one branch, not yet received at the otherNot yet
DamagedPhysically present and countable, but not fit to dispenseNo
Returned, pendingCame back from a customer, waiting to be inspectedNot yet

Why in-transit is its own status. When a van leaves the Mumbai warehouse for Ahmedabad, the stock is no longer at Mumbai and has not arrived at Ahmedabad. Systems that ignore this either double-count it or lose it for a day. Here it stays visible, owned by nobody, until someone receives it.

Expiry is not one of these. An expired batch is not a separate status — it is ordinary available stock whose batch has passed its date, which the allocation rule then refuses to pick and the expiry watchlist surfaces before it gets there. Writing it off posts a movement out of stock, so the loss is a ledger line with a reason rather than a quantity quietly changing.

Inventory · /movements

Movements

The ledger itself, unfiltered. Every single change to stock the system has ever recorded, newest first.

  • Every line says why. Goods receipt, sale issue, transfer dispatch, transfer receipt, adjustment, scrap, status change — the reason is part of the record, not a free-text note.
  • Every line says who. The user who caused it, and the document (purchase order, sales order, transfer) it came from.
  • Nothing here has an edit button. That is the point. A wrong entry is fixed by recording its reversal, so both the mistake and the correction stay visible forever.

Operations · /purchase-orders

Purchasing

Ordering stock from distributors, receiving it, and putting it away under the right batch and expiry date.

  1. Draft. Someone builds an order — a supplier, some products, some quantities. Nothing has happened to stock yet.
  2. Approved. A different person with the right permission approves it. The system refuses to let the same person do both.
  3. Received. When the boxes arrive, a goods receipt records what actually turned up, with each batch number and expiry date. This is the moment stock exists — and the moment the ledger gets its lines.

Why one person cannot do all three. Separating who asks for money to be spent from who agrees to spend it is the oldest control in accounting. The rule is enforced by the server, not by the screen hiding a button — and it is checked on identity, not on role, so an administrator who raised the order is refused exactly like anyone else. A separation of duties that a role can override is not a separation of duties.

Receiving goods — start from the order

The receipt form is deliberately order-first. You pick the approved purchase order before anything else, and the form fills itself in from it: the destination, the products, the quantities still outstanding and the price that was agreed when the order was placed.

What is left to type is only what the order genuinely cannot know — the supplier's invoice number, and the batch and expiry printed on the carton, which do not exist until the goods are manufactured.

  • Each line shows what the order still expects. Ordered, already received, outstanding. The quantity box starts at the outstanding figure, so the common case is to confirm rather than to type.
  • Short deliveries are normal, not an error. Receive 4 of 10 and the order becomes Partially received, with the arithmetic on the row — 4 of 10 — rather than a status word with nothing behind it. The remaining 6 stay outstanding for the next delivery.
  • Extra lines can be added. Distributors send free goods and samples that were never on the order; those get received alongside it rather than forcing someone to raise a fake order for them.
  • The destination is locked to the order. Receiving into the wrong branch is refused by the server. Left unchecked it books stock to a location that never saw it and closes an order that was never delivered — two wrong balances from one mis-click.
  • Batch and expiry are validated as you type, on the line that is wrong, for products that are batch-tracked. A line the server would reject is refused while you are still looking at the field.

What the colours mean

Status colour carries one meaning everywhere in the product, and purchasing is where it matters most. Green means finished. Amber means unfinished and yours to move. Blue means it is with somebody else.

That is why an approved order is amber rather than green. Approval is authorisation, not arrival — the goods are still on a lorry, and the order is still work waiting for you. It only turns green when everything ordered has actually been received.

Operations · /purchasing → New order

Scanning an invoice Live

Photograph a distributor's invoice and have fourteen lines of batch codes, expiry dates, quantities and rates fill themselves in — then check what the system says about its own reading.

What it does

Inside New order, once you have chosen a warehouse and a supplier, a Scan an invoice button appears. Give it a photograph or a PDF and it reads the document into the form: invoice number and date, and per line the product, pack, HSN code, batch number, expiry, quantity, rate and tax.

It creates nothing. There is no path from this feature to the stock ledger. What comes back is a filled-in form and a list of findings; a person still presses the same button they pressed before.

The reading is checked by arithmetic, not by trust

An invoice is an over-determined document, and that is what makes this safe. Quantity times rate must equal the line amount. The lines must sum to the subtotal. The fifteenth character of a GSTIN is a checksum over the other fourteen. None of those need an answer key — the paper checks itself.

So the system does not ask the model to be right. It asks it to produce something that can be checked, and then checks it. Findings are graded by what they could cause: BLOCK only where wrong stock could reach a shelf, REVIEW otherwise.

Worth trying: invoice 23. Its page carries two GSTINs — the supplier's and ours — and the reading takes one character from the wrong one. The result has the right shape, the right length and the right state code; no human would query it. The checksum does, because it is arithmetic. The finding says a character is wrong and deliberately offers no correction: recomputing the check digit would assume the other fourteen are right and hand you a second wrong number that now passes validation.

It learns each distributor's names

A distributor prints OMEZ-20 CAP where the catalogue says Omeprazole 20mg. Resolve it once and the system remembers that name for that supplier, so every later invoice from them matches exactly. Across the six demonstration invoices it resolves 70 of 73 lines; the three it refuses are ambiguous pack sizes, offered as a shortlist rather than guessed at.

How accurate it actually is

Measured on 50 generated invoices spanning different layouts, fonts, column sets, tax presentations and scan qualities — with the correct answer written first and the document rendered from it, so the answer key cannot itself be wrong.

88% of invoices are read with no errors at all — 44 of 50. The remaining six each miss one field, and every one of those is caught by the checks rather than passed through. Batch number, quantity and rate — the three fields where an error would put wrong stock on a shelf — are correct every single time, across 462 lines.

That result is the argument for the review step, not against the feature. Roughly one invoice in eight needs a correction: a queue a person can work through, and nowhere near good enough to book stock unattended.

No network? It still demonstrates. Six invoices and what the model read from each are recorded in the repository, keyed by a hash of the image so a reading is bound to the exact file that produced it. Only the transcription is replayed — the arithmetic, the checksum and the product matching all still run, because none of them ever made a network call.

Operations · /sales-orders

Sales

Orders going out — to hospitals and institutional buyers. The system picks which batches to send.

FEFO — first expiry, first out

When an order for 500 units is allocated, the system does not just take 500 from the pile. It sorts every batch of that product by expiry date and takes from the one expiring soonest, then the next, until the order is filled.

It is a small rule with a large effect: stock that would otherwise sit at the back of the shelf and expire gets sold first. Nobody has to remember to do it, and nobody can quietly not do it.

GST, split automatically

Indian sales tax splits differently depending on whether the buyer is in the same state. Within the state it becomes two half-rate components (CGST and SGST); across a state line it becomes one full-rate component (IGST). The system decides from the addresses and stores all three amounts on the invoice, so nobody has to know the rule.

Operations · /transfers

Transfers

Moving stock from the central warehouse to a branch, or between branches.

A transfer is deliberately two events, not one. Dispatch removes the stock from the sending location and marks it in transit. Receipt adds it at the destination. Between those two moments the stock is visible to everyone but available to nobody — which is exactly its real-world state, sitting in a van on the highway.

If the two counts disagree — 100 sent, 98 arrived — the difference does not vanish. It has to be resolved as an adjustment, with a reason.

Operations · /adjustments

Adjustments

The honest screen. Breakage, theft, expiry write-offs, and the corrections that follow a physical stock count.

  • Every adjustment needs a reason chosen from a fixed list — damaged, expired, lost, found on count, and so on. Free text is not enough to analyse later.
  • Every adjustment needs a second person. Nothing posts to the ledger until someone else approves it, for the same reason purchase orders work that way: an adjustment is the one screen that can make stock appear from nowhere.
  • Everything here is watched. The Exceptions screen reads adjustments closely — an unusual pattern of write-offs at one branch is exactly the sort of thing it is looking for.

Analysis · /replenishment

Replenishment Live

What to buy, how much, and why. This is the screen the whole system exists to make possible.

  • Ranked by urgency, not alphabetically. "Order today" means the stock will run out before a new delivery could arrive. "Order soon" means it will run out before the next time anyone looks at this screen.
  • Days of cover is the number to read first: how many days the current stock lasts at the rate this branch is actually selling.
  • Drafts are netted off. Once you raise a draft order, the row says drafted and stops suggesting the same units again — the single most common way replenishment tools cause double-ordering.
  • Raise draft order turns the suggestion into a real purchase order in draft, which still has to be approved by somebody. The system never places an order by itself.

How it decides — worked through

Open any row and the whole calculation is there. This is the real panel for Atorvastatin 10mg at the Bandra branch:

Read from the top, it is an ordinary chain of reasoning:

  1. This branch sells about 27.7 a day. That comes from the demand forecast, not from a flat average.
  2. This supplier usually takes 5 days. Measured from real purchase orders and receipts — not from what the supplier promised.
  3. So while waiting for a delivery, about 139 units will be sold. 27.7 × 5.
  4. But both of those numbers wobble. Some days sell more; some deliveries take longer. The system measures both wobbles and holds 101.7 units of safety stock to absorb them.
  5. Reorder point = 139 + 102 = 240. When stock drops below 240, it is time to order. Stock is 251, so this row is close but not urgent.
  6. Order enough to reach 434. That covers the wait for delivery plus the gap until somebody next looks at this screen. Subtract what is already in stock and on order, round up to whole packs — 190 units.

The one piece of real statistics

Safety stock is not a guess or a flat percentage. It combines two separate risks — demand being unusually high, and delivery being unusually late — using the standard formula Z × √(L σd² + d² σL²).

Z is how certain you want to be. The system uses a different value per drug class: 2.33 for cold-chain and controlled drugs (99% — the shelf empties early in fewer than one order cycle in a hundred), 1.88 for prescription medicines (97%), and 1.65 for everything else (95%). A critical drug simply gets more protection than a bottle of cough syrup, which is a business decision an administrator can change on the Settings screen.

Products that are too slow to model are skipped. Something selling three units a month has a lead-time demand under one unit, and any safety stock calculated for it is a rounding artefact dressed up as science. Those are better ordered by eye, and the system says so instead of inventing a number.

Analysis · /forecast

Demand forecast Live

How many units of each product each branch will need, day by day, for the next month.

Three methods, and a competition between them

Different products behave differently. Paracetamol at a hospital branch has a strong weekly rhythm; a slow-moving ointment has almost none. So rather than picking one method and hoping, the system runs three and lets the data choose:

MethodWhat it assumesGood at
Moving average Tomorrow looks like the last four weeks, flat Nothing much — it is the baseline every other method has to beat
Seasonal naïve Next Tuesday looks like recent Tuesdays Strong, stable weekly patterns
Holt-Winters A level, a trend that flattens out, and a weekly shape, all learned Most real products — it wins on most series here

The competition is honest

Each method is trained on all but the last four weeks of history, then asked to predict those four weeks — which it has never seen. The predictions are compared to what actually happened, and the method with the smallest error wins for that product at that branch. Then it is retrained on the full history to produce the real forecast.

This is why the "off by" column is trustworthy. It is not the error on data the model was fitted to, which would always look flattering. It is the error on data the model was deliberately kept away from.

Two details that matter more than the model

A stockout day is not a zero-demand day. If the shelf was empty on Tuesday, zero units were sold — but that is a fact about supply, not demand. Feeding those zeros to a forecast teaches it to expect less and order less, which causes the next stockout. The system detects days with no sellable stock, treats them as unknown, and fills them from the days around them. A gap longer than two weeks is left alone: at that point somebody decided to stop stocking the product, and inventing demand across it would be fiction.

The band widens as it goes. Predicting tomorrow is far easier than predicting day thirty, so the shaded band grows with the square root of how far ahead you look. A forecast drawn as a single confident line is lying about how much it knows.

Analysis · /exceptions

Exceptions Live

Movements that broke from the pattern around them — found by comparing the ledger against itself, not by a rule someone has to keep updating.

Four things it looks for

FindingThe question it asks
Demand breakDid this branch suddenly sell far more, or far less, of something than it had been?
ShrinkageIs one branch losing more stock to adjustments than its own recent history explains?
Large write-offIs this single loss big enough in rupees to be worth a question on its own?
Out of hoursDid stock move when the counter was shut?

How "unusual" is measured

The obvious approach — compare today to the average, flag anything far from it — fails in exactly the situation you care about. One enormous day drags the average up and inflates the spread, so the very outlier you are hunting hides itself.

So the system uses the middle value instead of the average, and the typical distance from that middle instead of the standard spread. Half the data would have to be corrupted before the measure moves — which means a single huge day stands out sharply rather than camouflaging itself.

The three refinements that made it usable

Compare against the last four weeks, not all of history. Judged against two years, every day of a seasonal peak looks like an anomaly. The first version produced 162 findings that were really just "it is flu season". Against a rolling four-week baseline — which contains four of every weekday — the weekly rhythm and the season are inside the comparison rather than being reported as news.

One event is one finding. A single recall wrote thirty movements at 11pm and produced thirty separate out-of-hours alerts. Movements close together in time are now grouped into one visit — the second row of the screenshot is "40 movements between 23:10 and 23:53", reported once.

Value can speak for itself. A branch that rarely writes anything off has no pattern to compare against, so a genuinely large loss there would score as statistically ordinary. Any write-off above a value threshold is reported regardless of the statistics.

Nothing here is stored. Findings are recomputed from the ledger every time the screen is opened, which means a finding disappears the moment the ledger stops supporting it — including when somebody reverses the bad movement that caused it. There is no list of stale alerts to maintain.

Analysis · /lead-times

Supplier lead times Live

How long each distributor actually takes — measured from your own purchase orders and goods receipts, not from what they promised.

Why the worst case matters more than the average

Two suppliers can both average five days. One always takes five. The other takes two days most of the time and twelve when it is busy. Ordering from them as if they were the same guarantees stockouts from the second one.

So each supplier gets two numbers. Usually is the middle of its deliveries. Worst case is the point nine out of ten deliveries beat. The gap between them is the cover needed — days of extra stock you are forced to carry purely because that supplier is unpredictable.

SupplierRatingUsuallyWorst caseCover neededMet promise
Apex Pharma SupplyErratic7d15d+8d49%
Gujarat Health TradersPredictable5d7d+2d71%
MedPlus DistributorsPredictable3d4d+1d71%

Across all three, 11 days of stock are being held purely to absorb their spread. That is a rupee figure sitting on a shelf, and it is the strongest argument anyone can take into a supplier negotiation.

Suppliers with too few deliveries are shown, but flagged as unproven, and their quoted lead time is used for ordering instead of a measured one. Five deliveries cannot support a percentile, and pretending otherwise is worse than admitting the gap.

Analysis · /ask

Ask a question Live

Type a question in plain English and get rows back — with the query that produced them attached, so you can check the answer rather than believe it.

The model does not answer your question

This is the part worth understanding, because it is what makes the feature safe to put in front of stock data. The model is given a description of the database and your question, and its only job is to write one SELECT statement. It never writes the answer. What appears on screen is rows PostgreSQL returned.

That statement then has to get past four things before it runs:

  1. A guard that refuses anything which is not a single read. No second statement, no UPDATE hiding in a WITH clause, no function that can touch a file or open a connection.
  2. The database itself. The statement is planned before it is executed, so PostgreSQL has agreed it is valid SQL against real columns.
  3. A read-only transaction, with a ten-second time limit and a cap of 200 rows. A query that would take the site down is stopped by the clock, not by good intentions.
  4. Your own permissions. A branch user gets the same branch they get everywhere else. Asking a question is not a way around who you are.

Why not simply instruct the model to behave? Because instructions in a prompt are a request, not a control — and people are inventive about talking models out of them. Nothing here relies on the model cooperating. The worst a cleverly worded question can achieve is to make it propose something destructive, which is then refused before anything reads it.

Every answer shows its working

Alongside the query, What it decided for you lists the judgement calls made in writing it: which branch a name was matched to, that reversals were excluded, that a total is in units because this database holds no selling price. Those are exactly the assumptions that turn an answer into a different answer, so they are written down rather than buried.

It will tell you when it cannot answer

Ask "which customers owe us money?" and it declines, in a sentence: this system records what moved, not what is owed, and there is no payments table anywhere in it. The tempting wrong answer was available — add up every order raised — and it would have named a walk-in customer who paid cash at the counter as a debtor.

The same applies to forecasts. Those figures are calculated when you open the Demand forecast screen and never stored, so a query for them is valid SQL that returns nothing — and "no rows" reads as "there is nothing to reorder", which is the most dangerous thing this system could tell you. It says so in words instead, and points at the screen that computes them.

If a question could reasonably mean two things, it asks which you meant rather than picking one quietly. A question handed back is a correct outcome here, not a failure.

Follow-ups, and why they are deliberately short

You can refine: "only the ones at Andheri", "and across the whole chain?", "who supplies it?". It carries the previous question and its query — and nothing older than that. Carrying more feels smarter and behaves worse: with a long history the system starts quietly dropping a filter you set three questions ago and hands back a smaller, entirely believable number that nobody thinks to question.

Being honest about accuracy. Fifty questions were put to this feature and every answer checked against a query written by hand. Nothing crashed; seven answers were wrong — each one a fact about this business that the database structure alone cannot state, such as the ledger recording what stock cost and never what it sold for. All seven were fixed by improving what the model is told, and re-checked. A question this feature has not been asked before can still produce a plausible wrong number, which is the honest reason the query sits under every answer.

Compliance · /recalls

Batch recalls

A manufacturer says batch PAR-650-B2 is contaminated. This screen freezes it everywhere at once and tells you who already received it.

This is the moment the whole ledger design pays for itself. Pressing that button:

  1. Quarantines every unit of that batch at every location simultaneously. The stock stays visible and counted — it simply cannot be dispensed or allocated to any order.
  2. Traces where it already went. Because every issue and transfer of that batch is a ledger line, the system can list each customer and branch that received units, with quantities and dates — in seconds, not days.
  3. Records the regulator's reference alongside the reason, so the paperwork and the system agree.

Why this is impossible without a ledger. A system that only stores a current quantity per product can tell you how many units you have. It cannot tell you which of them came from the contaminated batch, or who you sold the rest to. That answer only exists if you kept every line.

Compliance · /audit

Audit trail

Every state change in the system, who made it, and what the record looked like immediately before.

The Movements screen records what happened to stock. This records what happened to everything else — a supplier's phone number being edited, a purchase order being approved, a user's role being changed, a setting being moved. Each entry keeps a copy of the record before and after, so any change can be reconstructed exactly.

It is visible to administrators only, and it is the one screen in the product with no create, edit or delete action of any kind.

Setup · /master-data

Master data

Distributors, customers, locations and the classifications products hang off.

  • Suppliers carry the lead time they promise and their payment terms. The Analysis layer compares that promise against what they actually did.
  • Customers carry an address, which is what decides how GST splits on their invoices.
  • Locations are the central warehouse and five branches across two states, each with its own shelves and cold-chain storage. Two states is not decoration — it is what makes the interstate GST path real rather than theoretical.
  • Records are retired, never deleted. A supplier you stop using is marked inactive — deleting it would orphan two years of purchase orders and break the history everything else depends on.

Setup · /users

Users

Who can sign in, what they are allowed to do, and which branch they see.

Each person gets a role, and each role holds a set of 28 individual permissions — po.approve, stock.view_cost, audit.view and so on. Counter staff can additionally be pinned to one branch, so they only ever see their own stock.

Setup · /settings

Settings

The judgement calls behind the Analysis screens, and the switch for each capability. Administrators only.

Capabilities

Each of the four Analysis features has a switch. Turning one off closes its API as well as hiding its menu item — the endpoint starts answering "this feature is switched off" rather than merely disappearing from the navigation. That distinction is the whole point: something misbehaving in front of an audience has to actually stop.

The two unbuilt features appear here too, marked Not built yet and impossible to switch on. An honest roadmap beats a menu item that fails.

Three guardrails

Bad values are refused, not stored. Every setting declares its type and its range once, in one place, and both the form and the server enforce it from the same declaration.

Combinations are checked too. Some pairs are individually legal and jointly meaningless — a closing time before the opening time, a test window longer than the history it is held out of, a "critical" service level lower than the ordinary one. Those are rejected with an explanation.

Nothing half-saves. If any field in a batch is wrong, the whole batch is rejected and what you see on screen is still exactly what is in effect. Every change is recorded in the audit trail with its before and after values, so "it started recommending twice as much last Tuesday" is an answerable question.

What is deliberately not a setting. Anything that could make a number wrong rather than different. The append-only ledger, the first-expiry-first-out rule, the GST split and the two-person approval rule are not adjustable and never will be — they are the guarantees the system exists to make. What is adjustable is judgement: how cautious to be, how far back to look, how hard to chase a stockout.

Architecture

Everything from here on is for whoever has to build on this, review it, or run it. It is written to be readable without the code open.

The shape of it

Four pieces, one machine. There is no message broker, no separate cache, no search cluster and no vector database — each of those was considered and left out, because every extra moving part is another thing that can be down at 2am for a chain with five branches.

→
CaddyReverse proxy, automatic HTTPS certificates
→
FastAPIPython, 97 endpoints, OpenAPI generated automatically
→
PostgreSQL 1642 tables, the single source of truth

Three layers, one direction

The backend is stacked so that dependencies only ever point downward. Nothing in a lower layer knows the layer above it exists.

Layer 0 — the record app/models, app/services

Products, batches, locations, the movement ledger and the balance projection. Owns every invariant: append-only, batch integrity, GST arithmetic, the audit log.

↑

Layer 1 — the documents app/api/v1

Purchase orders, goods receipts, sales orders, transfers, adjustments, recalls. Each one is a state machine whose transitions write ledger lines. Nothing here writes stock directly; it all goes through Layer 0.

↑

Layer 2 — the analysis app/ai

Forecasting, replenishment, exceptions, lead times. Read-only by construction — these modules import query helpers and never the write services, so a bug here cannot corrupt the record.

Why the build order was 0 → 1 → 2. Layer 2 is the interesting part, but it is worthless without two years of real movement history to read. So the ledger came first, then the documents that fill it, then a synthetic history generator that produced ~53,000 movements across 730 days with genuine weekly rhythm, seasonal illness peaks, supplier delays and stockouts — and only then the analysis that reads them.

Reference

The data model

The ledger and its projection

stock_movements is append-only: no update path, no delete path, in the code or in the permissions. Every row carries the product, the batch, the location, the signed quantity, the movement type, the document it came from, the user, and the timestamp.

stock_balances is a projection of that ledger — a running total per product, per batch, per location, per status. It is maintained by a database trigger that fires on insert, so it cannot drift out of step with the ledger even if a write comes from outside the application.

Document transitione.g. a goods receipt is posted
→
INSERT into stock_movementsone row per batch received
→
Trigger firesAFTER INSERT, inside the same transaction
→
stock_balances updatedrebuildable from the ledger at any time

A test in the suite recomputes every balance from scratch and asserts it matches the projection. If that test ever fails, the projection is wrong and the ledger is right — and the fix is to rebuild the projection, never to edit the ledger.

Other decisions worth knowing

Batch and expiry tracking

Stock is keyed by batch, not just by product. That is what makes first-expiry-first-out allocation and single-batch recalls possible. Cold-chain products additionally carry storage constraints that mark them as critical in the replenishment maths.

Concurrency — two people selling the last box

Allocation takes a row-level database lock on the batch rows it is about to consume. Two simultaneous sales of the same last units cannot both succeed; the second one waits, re-reads, and correctly reports that stock ran out. Without this, the ledger would happily record a negative balance.

GST — three columns, not one

Every invoice line stores CGST, SGST and IGST separately rather than a single tax figure. Which of them is non-zero is decided by comparing the seller's and buyer's state. Storing the split rather than recomputing it means a historical invoice still reads correctly after tax rules change.

Audit log — before and after

Every state change writes an audit row containing a JSON snapshot of the record before and after, the action name, the actor and the timestamp. Settings changes, approvals, master-data edits and user changes all land here.

Time zones — stored in UTC, asked in local time

Every timestamp is stored in UTC. Every question — "was this out of hours?", "how much sold on Tuesday?" — is asked in Asia/Kolkata. Getting this wrong is not a cosmetic bug: an early version treated 20:00 UTC as an evening hour when it is 01:30 in Mumbai, and produced 1,698 false out-of-hours findings. The conversion now lives in one module that everything else calls.

Caching the expensive parts

Fitting sixty forecast models takes a few seconds, so results are cached in process against two things: the highest movement id in the ledger, and a version counter that every settings change increments. Add a movement or change a setting and the cache is invalid immediately — there is no stale window. The models are also fitted once at start-up in a background thread, so the first person to open the screen does not pay for it.

Running it

In production

Everything runs in Docker on a single AWS EC2 instance inside the free tier — an ARM machine with 2 GB of memory. Caddy sits in front and obtains TLS certificates by itself. Postgres runs as a container with a mounted volume. Four services in total, and a one-shot migration step that must succeed before the API is allowed to start, so the application can never serve against a half-migrated schema.

Merging to the main branch deploys. Images are built on GitHub's own ARM machines — never on the server, which does not have the memory for a frontend build — published to the container registry tagged with the commit, then pulled and restarted. Every image carries its commit hash, so a rollback is "deploy the previous one" rather than "rebuild and hope". A final check calls the public URL from outside the network, because a check run on the server itself only proves the containers started, not that anybody can reach them.

One decision worth explaining

The deploy step runs on a runner installed on the server itself, which reaches out to GitHub rather than being reached. The obvious alternative was to have GitHub connect in over SSH — but SSH is open only to one address, and GitHub's machines come from thousands that rotate.

The choice was to open the SSH port to the whole internet, or to have the server ask for work instead. It asks. Nothing new is exposed, and no server key or cloud credential is stored in GitHub at all.

The tight memory budget shaped real decisions: no broker, no separate cache, and a forecasting stack that is numpy and statsmodels rather than anything requiring a gigabyte of tensors.

On a laptop

Nothing is installed system-wide. Postgres runs from a directory inside the project on a non-standard port, so it cannot collide with anything else on the machine, and deleting the project directory removes it completely.

  1. Start the database and apply migrations.
  2. Seed it — roles, permissions, users, products, locations, suppliers.
  3. Generate history — 730 days of synthetic movements, which is what makes the Analysis screens meaningful.
  4. Run the API and the web app, and sign in with one of the demo accounts.

Reference

Testing

494 tests run against a live API and a real database. They deliberately do not assert that the forecast is accurate — pinning an error rate in a test only makes the suite brittle. They assert the properties that cause real damage when they break:

  • The ledger invariant — balances recomputed from movements match the projection exactly.
  • First-expiry-first-out — allocation takes the earliest-expiring batch, every time.
  • GST — the split is correct within a state and across a state line.
  • Every permission boundary — each role is checked against each protected endpoint, including the separation-of-duties rule.
  • Analysis safety — a forecast never predicts negative demand; percentiles are ordered; stock already on order or on a draft is netted off so nothing is ordered twice; prices come from the supplier record and not from the request.
  • Settings — bad values are refused, contradictory combinations are refused, a batch with one bad field writes none of it, a switched-off feature closes its own endpoint and no others, and every change lands in the audit log.
  • The two halves still agree. The browser's copy of the API contract is generated from the API itself, and the build regenerates it and fails if the committed copy has fallen behind. Renaming a field on the server is therefore a compile error, not a column that silently renders blank.

Why the tests need a real server and a real database. The properties worth protecting here are database behaviour — the trigger that refuses to let a movement be edited, the row locks that stop two people selling the same last box, the projection that has to match the ledger. None of them survive being faked, so the suite starts an actual API against an actual Postgres and talks to it over HTTP, exactly as the browser does.

Glossary

Every term in this guide that is not ordinary English, explained without assuming anything.

Batch (or lot)

A manufacturing run, identified by a code printed on the box, with its own expiry date. Two batches of the same medicine are not interchangeable — which is why recalls work on batches, not products.

SKU

Stock Keeping Unit — the code identifying a sellable product, like PAR-650 for Paracetamol 650mg.

FEFO

First Expiry, First Out. When filling an order, take from the batch that expires soonest. Prevents stock quietly ageing at the back of the shelf.

Quarantined

Physically present but not sellable — usually because the batch is under recall or waiting on a quality check.

In transit

Dispatched from one location, not yet received at the next. Counted, visible, and available to neither.

Drug schedule

India's classification of medicines. Schedule H and H1 require a prescription; Schedule X is tightly controlled. The system protects higher-schedule stock more carefully.

Cold chain

Products that must stay refrigerated end to end. Treated as critical, because a break costs the whole batch.

GST / CGST / SGST / IGST

India's sales tax. Within a state it splits into central and state halves (CGST + SGST); across a state line it is a single integrated tax (IGST).

Lead time

How long a supplier takes between an order being placed and the stock arriving. Measured here from your own records, not from the supplier's promise.

Days of cover

How many days the current stock will last at the rate this branch is actually selling. The first number to read on the Replenishment screen.

Reorder point

The stock level at which you must order to avoid running out before the delivery arrives. Demand during the wait, plus safety stock.

Safety stock

Extra stock held to absorb two kinds of bad luck: an unusually busy week, and an unusually late delivery. Measured separately for each and combined.

Service level

How certain you want to be of not running out. 95% means that in five order cycles out of a hundred, the shelf empties before the delivery lands. Higher certainty costs cash tied up in stock.

Review period

How often somebody actually looks at the Replenishment screen. Orders are sized to last until the next look, so setting it shorter than reality causes stockouts between reviews.

Percentile / P90

The value that nine out of ten observations come in under. A supplier's P90 lead time is its realistic worst case, which matters far more than its average.

Backtest

Hiding the recent past from a model, asking it to predict that period, and scoring it against what really happened. The only honest way to compare forecasting methods.

Holt-Winters

A forecasting method that learns three things at once: the current level, a trend that gradually flattens, and a repeating weekly shape.

Stockout

Having none of something that people wanted. Critically, a stockout day is a day of unknown demand, not zero demand.

Shrinkage

Stock that disappears without a sale — breakage, theft, miscounting. The Exceptions screen watches for branches losing more than their own history explains.

Append-only ledger

A record where lines are only ever added, never edited or removed. Corrections are new lines that reverse old ones, so the history of a mistake survives alongside its fix.

Projection

A summary table derived from a ledger and kept up to date automatically. Fast to read, and always rebuildable from the lines it came from.

Separation of duties

The person who creates something cannot be the person who approves it. Applied here to purchase orders, transfers and adjustments.
